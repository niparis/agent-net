A network of gateways

How agent-net goes from one owner with one gateway to many owners whose gateways talk to each other, without giving anyone outside the power to spend your money or speak in your name.

Each identity has a master node and some child nodes. The master node is the gateway. The child nodes are that owner's machines, each running a daemon. Only master nodes talk to each other.

The decision in six lines

FederationEach owner keeps one gateway. Gateways link to each other. Machines of different owners never talk.
A new kind of peerA foreign gateway has its own listener, its own key and its own trust label. It never holds a machine token.
Messages onlyThe federation listener has no operation that starts or wakes a session or a worker.
One front deskOutside messages for the owner enter the front door already approved in decision 0150.
Identity by signatureA gateway key, plus an optional sign-in. Each gateway checks both signatures itself.
Later stagesPublic discovery and zero-knowledge proofs come last. Sharing with named people needs neither.

Identity: how others know you are not lying

Each gateway makes one key pair for federation. The public key is its network identity. It is separate from machine enrollment, which stays as it is.

An owner may also sign in on their gateway with an issuer such as Google. When gateway B says hello to gateway A, it sends its key and the sign-in token, and signs the whole hello. Gateway A checks two things itself:

  1. The hello is signed by the key it claims.
  2. The token is signed by the issuer. A fetches the issuer's public keys from the issuer's own address.

If both hold, A knows that the holder of this gateway key signed in as this person. The only thing trusted is the issuer, the same as "Sign in with Google" on any website.

  • Tokens are short-lived and bound to the gateway key, so a copied token is useless elsewhere.
  • Each owner chooses which issuers their gateway accepts.
  • Without sign-in, sharing still works with a gateway key in place of an email.

Someone who may see a repo can do three things: send messages to it, clone it with git, and copy its settings to their own gateway. They cannot start work on your gateway. Work happens on theirs.

The answer for a repo you may not see is the same as for a repo that does not exist.

Outside messages and the front desk

A message from another gateway gets its own label, outside request. The gateway sets the label from where the message came in, never from what the message says.

  • It never carries an approval note. "Your user approved", owner words and peer trust do not apply to it.
  • The receiving repo lead judges it under its charter. With nothing said in the charter, the lead may answer questions and must not do work.
  • It cannot wake a session by itself.

A message addressed to a gateway, and not to one of its repos, is a message to its human. It enters the front door of decision 0150: the chief of staff sorts it and the Desk shows what needs you. The chief sees the outside label and the checked identity of the sender.

The front desk depends on decision 0150, which is approved and not yet built.

Shared worker-quality counts

  • Unit: one row per picker cell: harness, model with version, effort, difficulty level.
  • Content: runs, accepted, closed, rework points and the time window. Raw counts, so a receiver can combine them with its own.
  • Cost: per accepted result at public list prices, never your real spend.
  • Never shared: brief text, repo names, session ids, machine timings, quota levels.
  • Use: a starting guess where the receiver has little data. Its own results replace it.

Zero-knowledge proofs

Not needed for sharing with a named person: you already know the name, and the person proves it to you only.

They become useful in one case: a repo shared with a group, where a member wants to prove "I am on the list" to other members without showing which entry they are. That can be added later on top of the same sign-in token.

Open questions

  1. Reach. A gateway that receives needs an address others can reach. Is Tailscale Funnel acceptable, as the apps listener already uses, or must a gateway with no public address be able to take part by polling?
  2. Public and messages. May anyone message a public repo, or only people who signed in? The draft lets anyone, with the charter deciding and the front desk sorting.
  3. The directory. Who runs the first one: you, or nobody until a second owner exists?
  4. Several master nodes. Can one identity have more than one gateway, for example home and work? The draft assumes one.
